00Thesis

Hackathons are not exams.

Most developers lose because they treat them like school exams — 90% of their time on invisible infrastructure, 10% on why anyone should care. Before Web3, Matt signed a pro MLG esports contract at 14 and generated over $1M in door-to-door sales. Both taught the same brutal truth: nobody cares how hard you worked if they cannot understand what you built in 15 seconds.

Assume your hardware dies at the worst moment. His crashed mid-hackathon. Push to remote constantly — and cut the demo video four hours before the deadline, not four minutes.

01The momentum effect

Wins stop being lucky breaks.

First Superteam Earn bounty landed on day three living in Asia. First major win at the $100,000 Render hackathon on day 28. Once the formula clicks: 1st places at dFlow × Superteam Thailand and Superteam UK × HelpBnk, the Filecoin track at muShanghai, the OmniAI voice hackathon, three Breakpoint London wins, 2nd on the Superteam website hackathon (which got him invited onto their dev team) — plus sprint wins with Curious Ventures × Arc, Crossbar, and Popup.

02Picking battles

EV math first, mission filter second.

  • Go where your community knows your face. Nearly every win started through a local community, Superteam chapter, or Superteam Earn — not the 1,500-competitor global track.
  • Hunt the side tracks. Sponsors pay thousands for tracks that draw 10–20 serious submissions. In one Colosseum day he entered 5 sponsor side tracks; in one 24-hour window he submitted 7 bounties and won 3.
  • Read the rubric like a lawyer. Half the field disqualifies itself — skipped SDK integration, blown video limit.
  • Build the feature your own product needs anyway. Low competition plus something you'd have built regardless is the only combination that compounds, or you abandon the repo by week three.
03Preparation

You win it in the three weeks prior.

  • Point one core loop at the new sponsor tech before the clock starts — what does their SDK make faster, safer, better?
  • Treat the demo video as the product. Judges have 60 tabs open. Problem first, live UI by frame three, transactions working on camera. Forty-five seconds of login screen and you've lost.
  • Rehearse out loud to real humans — pitch workshops, demo days, builder houses.
04People

Talk to the room, not the repo.

  • The sponsor's DevRel & track owner wrote the criteria. Day one: "here's how I'm integrating your SDK — is this what winning looks like to you?"
  • Stress-test their SDK and file it. Sponsors use events to shake out beta tooling; a documented bug or PR is the cheapest proof of competence in the room.
  • Mentors and judges, before and after. They're product owners giving up a weekend — connect over the problem, thank them by name.
  • Paying strangers beat the scorecard once: 53 people paid $5–$10 in three hours for early access to a product that didn't exist yet. Real traction beats judging sheets.
05Relationships compound

Show up with proof of work.

He won a website bounty and got invited onto the dev team. Found a teammate who matched his pace for the Filecoin win. Lived around a Da Nang builder house surrounded by daily shippers. Tagged the ecosystem on every real update until Superteam and Solana's own accounts featured the work.

06What wins

One loop, not a feature list.

The graveyard of losing repos always looks the same: a landing page, 14 half-finished buttons, broken features. Operator Uplift is one bet with a verdict — pick a daily commitment, prove it with an AI-verified photo, stake a refundable deposit in escrow; keep your word and split what quitters forfeited. Every other build in the record (NS LevelUp, Agentic Escrow, Velocity AI) is the same shape: a single complete user loop, start to finish.

07The solo toolkit

One person, nine repos.

  • AI-assisted engineering — subagents, parallel worktrees with verification gates, Playwright suites, property tests.
  • Motion design & video — launch trailers that feel like Tier-1 consumer drops.
  • Sales psychology — every pitch framed on human stakes and loss aversion.
  • Daily distribution — build in public every day, so judges already know the product exists.
08Do's & don'ts

The checklist.

DO

  • Ship a live URL or APK a judge tests in 10 seconds, zero .env.
  • Stack side tracks on the same architecture.
  • Answer: can this sponsor pilot it next quarter?
  • Keep shipping the morning after the deadline.
  • Turn every trophy into a grant, users, or distribution.

DON'T

  • Build a feature list.
  • Wait for a co-founder — fast solo beats slow committee.
  • Abandon the repo after judging.
  • Launch a token as a shortcut. Product first.
